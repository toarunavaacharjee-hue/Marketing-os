Most battlecards fail for one reason: they're built by marketing, handed to sales, and never touched again. Six months later the competitive landscape has shifted, the pricing is wrong, and the talk track references a feature that was deprecated in Q1.

A battlecard only works if it's built to be used — short enough to read in two minutes before a call, specific enough to handle a real objection, and maintained on a schedule. This template gives you the structure and the process.

## What a battlecard is

A battlecard is a one-page competitive reference for a specific competitor. It gives sales and customer-facing teams the key facts, talking points, and objection responses they need to win a deal where that competitor is in the mix.

It is not:
- A full competitive analysis (that's for the product team)
- A features comparison table (that's for the website)
- A brand positioning document (that's for marketing)

A battlecard is a sales tool. Every section should answer the question: "What does a sales rep need to know in the next two minutes before joining a call?"

## The battlecard template

### 1. Competitor snapshot (2-3 sentences)
What they are, who they target, how they position. Write this as if briefing a new hire who has never heard of them.

### 2. When you win
The specific deal types, segments, and situations where you reliably beat this competitor. Two to four bullets. Be specific: "Deals where the buyer has a dedicated PMM team and needs more than one ICP segment" beats "enterprise deals."

### 3. When you lose
Honest. The segments, use cases, or price points where they currently win. If your team doesn't know this, they can't handle the conversation.

### 4. Their top 3 claims (and your counter)
Format each as:

> **Their claim:** [Exact language from their website/sales deck]
> **Your counter:** [One to two sentences with a proof point]

Don't argue about the feature. Redirect to the outcome that matters for the ICP.

### 5. Objection responses
The three to four objections a rep is most likely to hear. Format:

> **Objection:** "We're already using [Competitor X]."
> **Response:** [1-2 sentences that acknowledge, then redirect to a dimension where you win]

### 6. Key differentiators (3 bullets max)
The shortest version of why you, not them. This is what goes on a whiteboard or in a one-line response in a chat. Each bullet: one claim + one proof point.

### 7. Traps to avoid
Things your team should NOT say. Common mistakes that play into the competitor's strength or make you look defensive.

### 8. Resources
- Relevant case study or proof point
- Win story from your CRM (name-blind)
- Link to competitive FAQ for deeper dives

## Who uses battlecards (and what they use them for)

Battlecards aren't just for sales. Across marketing and sales functions:

- **Account executives:** Pre-call prep, live objection handling
- **SDRs and BDRs:** Handling competitive mentions in outbound responses
- **Customer success:** Renewal conversations where a competitor is pitching the account
- **Demand gen and campaign managers:** Writing ad copy and landing pages that address competitive claims without naming competitors
- **Content marketers:** Informing comparison posts, "why switch" pages, and competitor-aware SEO content
- **Product marketing:** Tracking which competitive claims are landing vs. being rebutted

The version sales uses should be shorter and more action-oriented than the one marketing uses to brief leadership. One template, two levels of depth.

## How to keep battlecards current

Build a maintenance cadence into the process:

- **Quarterly:** Full review of all claims, pricing, and proof points
- **On competitor announcement:** Update the affected battlecard within one week
- **After win/loss reviews:** Feed new objections and counters into the relevant cards
- **After a lost deal:** Debrief what the competitor said and whether the rep had the right response

If nobody owns the battlecard, it decays. Assign one owner per competitor in marketing. For how to run a structured competitive analysis that feeds the cards, see the [competitive messaging framework](/blog/competitive-messaging-framework-b2b-saas).

## Common battlecard mistakes

- **Too long:** More than one page and sales won't read it before calls
- **Feature-focused:** Your feature list vs. their feature list is not a sales argument
- **No proof:** "We're better at X" without a quote, metric, or win story is not a counter
- **Not updated after product launches:** If you shipped a feature that closes a gap, update the relevant battlecards the week it ships
