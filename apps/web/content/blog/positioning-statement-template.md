Most positioning statements end up in a brand deck nobody reads. They're written once, approved, and forgotten — while the actual positioning decisions get made ad hoc by whoever's writing the next campaign or pitch.

A positioning statement is only useful if it makes daily decisions easier. This template is designed for exactly that: defining your category, differentiation, and core claim in a format that travels across teams.

## What a positioning statement is (and what it isn't)

A positioning statement defines how you want your product perceived, by whom, against what alternatives, and on what dimension. It is internal — a working constraint for teams, not copy for customers.

It is not:
- A tagline (that's a distilled, external expression of positioning)
- A mission statement (that's about the company, not the product)
- A value proposition (that's the what; positioning is the why-better)

For a deeper look at the difference, see [positioning vs messaging](/blog/positioning-vs-messaging).

## The positioning statement template

Use this structure:

> **For** [target audience] **who** [need or trigger], **[product name]** is a **[category]** that **[primary benefit / differentiator]**. **Unlike** [main alternative], **[product]** [key proof point or distinction].

Fill in each field from evidence, not aspiration:

- **Target audience:** Your primary ICP — one segment, not all buyers. If your ICP isn't defined, see the [ICP framework](/blog/ideal-customer-profile-icp-framework).
- **Need or trigger:** What makes them look now? A pain event, a growth inflection, a product launch, a sales team that keeps improvising. Be specific.
- **Category:** Where you live in the buyer's mind. Define it too narrowly and you shrink the market. Too broadly and you mean nothing.
- **Primary benefit / differentiator:** The one thing you do better than anything else for this segment. Not a feature — an outcome.
- **Alternative:** How they solve this today. Direct competitor, incumbent, or "doing nothing." Be honest.
- **Key proof:** Metric, case study hook, or speed claim that makes the benefit credible.

## How to use this positioning statement day to day

Once it's written, the positioning statement becomes a filter:

- **For demand gen and campaign managers:** Campaign messaging should be a direct expression of the primary benefit for the ICP trigger. If a campaign headline can't be traced back to the positioning, it probably shouldn't run.
- **For content marketers:** Every pillar piece and SEO article should reinforce the category claim. If you're positioning as a marketing OS, your content strategy proves what a marketing OS does.
- **For sales:** The differentiation column in the positioning statement is the talk track. "Unlike X, we..." is not a slogan — it's the objection script.
- **For brand and design:** The category framing shapes tone, visual language, and the persona your creative speaks to.

## When to update your positioning statement

- You expand to a new ICP segment — the trigger, benefit, and alternative all change
- A competitor claims your differentiation — you need to move to a new dimension
- You ship a feature that changes the primary benefit — your proof points are outdated
- Win rate drops significantly — usually a positioning or proof problem, not a copy problem

## Common positioning mistakes

- **Writing for everyone:** "For businesses that want to grow" — means nothing, guides nothing
- **Leading with the feature:** "The only tool with AI-powered message generation" — a feature is not a position
- **Vague differentiation:** "Better, faster, smarter" without a proof point — every competitor says this
- **Internal language in the category:** If customers don't call themselves "GTM teams," they won't find you
